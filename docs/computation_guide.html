Rewarding Doubt · TriviaQA · Llama-3-8B · 3–5 seeds per arm

Rewarding doubt without KL

The released Rewarding Doubt recipe trains the confidence a model states after its answer with PPO and a KL penalty toward the base model. Two independent changes improve it. Replacing the KL penalty with a format hinge gives most of the calibration gain. Scoring all eleven confidence levels exactly, instead of one sampled level, gives most of the ranking gain and makes training far more repeatable. With both methods tuned on the same budget and trained on the same answer labels, discrete-exact with the hinge reaches test ECE 0.052, AUROC 0.839 and Brier 0.155, against 0.114, 0.721 and 0.203 for PPO with the hinge.

Two independent choices

Every run shares the released code's front half. The model reads a TriviaQA question, writes Answer: …, Confidence, and the answer is graded correct or wrong. Only the few confidence tokens that follow are trained. Two choices remain.

  • How is the confidence scored? PPO samples one confidence string and learns from its reward. Discrete-exact reads the probability of all eleven levels (0 to 10) from one forward pass and optimizes the expected reward exactly.
  • What keeps the model in line? The released recipe uses a KL penalty toward the base model plus a −30 reward for malformed output. The alternative is a format hinge that acts only when the output format slips.

We ran all four combinations with three seeds each, on the same 1,024 training and 512 held-out questions, with the same learning rate and number of updates.

How firm is this 2×2? Two later checks qualify it.

  • Training labels differed. The released PPO code grades answers by exact match during training, while discrete-exact (and every evaluation) uses word-overlap F1 > 0.5, as the paper describes. Exact match rejects about 5% of correct answers. Re-grading the saved outputs by exact match flips the ECE ordering between the two methods, so the ECE gap between PPO and discrete-exact in this figure is confounded by the label. The hinge-vs-KL effect within each method, and discrete-exact's AUROC and stability advantages, hold under both graders. The grading report (docs/grading.md in the repository) lists every disagreement.
  • PPO's seeds were not really seeded. TRL resets the random seed to its config default, so these three PPO runs differed only through GPU nondeterminism. They are still independent runs, and their wide spread is real run-to-run variation.

The tuned comparison below fixes both: every arm trains on F1 labels and PPO is properly seeded.

Why the hinge helps

The base model is the thing being fixed. It says "10" for most answers, right or wrong. A KL penalty charges the trained model for every departure from the base model's distribution, including the departure calibration requires, so it keeps pulling probability back toward high confidence. The hinge only asks that the output stay well formed: at least 95% of the probability goes to : followed by a number, and the model stops after the number. Within that, it leaves the choice of number entirely to the calibration objective.

What the hinge bought

  • Calibration. ECE fell from 0.127 to 0.070 for PPO and from 0.085 to 0.057 for discrete-exact. Discrete-exact with the hinge was the best and steadiest arm (0.050–0.065 across seeds).
  • Tighter format. In training, 99.7% of sampled confidences were a valid number with the hinge, against 90–94% with KL and the −30 penalty.
  • No reference model. KL needs a second forward pass through the base model every step; the hinge reuses quantities the training pass already computes.
  • Fewer settings. No KL coefficient, target or controller; one weight and one threshold that matter only when the format fails.
  • No sign of drift. The usual reason for KL is to stop the model wandering off. Answer accuracy and evaluation format were unchanged without it.

What it costs

  • A little ranking for discrete-exact. AUROC was 0.789 with the hinge against 0.808 with KL, possibly because KL preserves some of the base model's ordering. For PPO the hinge improved AUROC too.
  • No general guard. The adapter is shared by all tokens, so in principle it could change the answers. It did not here. Generating the answer with the adapter switched off would rule it out by construction.

Why scoring all levels helps

PPO learns from one sampled confidence per question, so its update depends on which level it happened to draw, and a learned value head is needed to tame the noise. Discrete-exact computes the expected reward over all eleven levels, so the calibration signal involves no sampling. Every level from 0 to 10 is a single token in Llama-3, so one forward pass gives all eleven probabilities.

The exact gradient pushes each level by πk·(Rk − J): its reward relative to the expected reward J. That has the ideal baseline built in, which is the job PPO's value head does approximately. The payoff shows in the 2×2: higher AUROC in both columns and a three- to five-fold smaller spread across seeds.

A value head still helps as an auxiliary task. Trained on top of discrete-exact (with KL), it raised AUROC to 0.826 ± 0.002, the most consistent arm, at a small calibration cost (ECE 0.097 ± 0.017). Combining it with the hinge has not been run yet.

Tuned and matched

Every run above used the paper's learning rate, 1e-5. Both methods were then tuned on the same budget: a separate 512-question dev split (disjoint from test), 16 random configurations per method, then four one-at-a-time sweeps (learning rate, updates per batch, learning rate again, regularization settings), always keeping the configuration with the lowest dev Brier. The winners were rerun with fresh seeds and scored once on test.

The search turned up a failure mode the earlier runs never reached: at high learning rates the adapter damages the answers themselves, not just the confidence, because it applies to every token. Some configurations dropped answer accuracy to 0–30% or broke the output format, and plain Brier ranked them highly because a model that is mostly wrong and says "low" scores well. Selection therefore only considered runs whose format failures stayed under 2% and whose accuracy stayed within 2 points of the base model. The hinge guards the format, not the answers.

Both preferred fewer updates per batch than the paper's 8, and discrete-exact tolerated a learning rate nearly twice PPO's before its answers degraded.
  • Discrete-exact wins on all three metrics. The AUROC gap (0.12) is more than five standard errors; the ECE gap (0.06) is about two.
  • PPO's problem is variance. Its five seeds range from 0.044 to 0.163 ECE. Its best seed matches discrete-exact's typical run; its average does not. The search itself was misled by this: PPO's tuning seed happened to be a good one (dev ECE 0.025), and fresh seeds did not reproduce it.
  • The label was not the cause. The same tuned PPO trained on exact-match labels gave test ECE 0.128 and AUROC 0.757, within noise of the F1-trained runs.
  • Tuning helped discrete-exact mainly on ranking. Against its untuned three seeds (ECE 0.057, AUROC 0.789, Brier 0.174), calibration barely moved while AUROC and Brier improved.

PPO's original cost was implementation, not algorithm. TRL computes a full-vocabulary log-softmax at every position, logs an entropy statistic over the same logits, keeps gradient checkpointing on and clears the GPU cache every step. Patching those from outside the released code changes nothing numerically: over six steps on the same seed, rewards, losses, KL, clip fraction and value predictions matched to four decimals. At equal speed, any comparison can give both methods the same budget.

Next

  • Mixing in the Brier score (running). The log score punishes a confident mistake so hard that discrete-exact never says 9 or 10, even where it is right 82–88% of the time at 7–8. A reward of half log score and half Brier score, both proper, let it use 9 at 94% accuracy and nearly halved dev ECE in an exploratory sweep (2 seeds, half the training set). A finer sweep and a full three-seed comparison against the pure log score are running.
  • Hinge plus value head for discrete-exact.
  • Answer generation with the adapter off, so only the confidence can change and high learning rates cannot damage the answers.

Appendix: the fractional reward

We also tried scoring the mean confidence, R(Σk πk·k/10, y), instead of the expected reward. It is a principled target: its optimum makes the mean equal the true chance of being right, between grid points if needed. We dropped it for three reasons.

  • It calibrates the mean, not what the model writes. Matched to the baseline on Llama, its sampled integers had ECE 0.170 ± 0.023 while its mean readout had 0.090 ± 0.005. The objective lets the distribution stay spread, and sampling at temperature 0.6 turns that spread into miscalibrated integers.
  • It broke the output format without a guard. On Qwen3 without the hinge, its valid-number probability collapsed to 0.026 within two epochs.
  • It was unstable. On Llama its ECE fell fastest early (0.031 at step 64) and then rose to 0.099 before recovering.

Read from the full distribution it did rank answers best of anything tested (AUROC 0.840 ± 0.009), so it remains an option when the confidence can be read as a probability rather than sampled as text.
